Purpose of this guide

Why this exists

Most project management advice is written for large corporates with dedicated PMOs, enterprise software and full-time project managers. Irish small businesses and charities rarely have any of that — instead they have a manager wearing five hats, a handful of staff or volunteers, a tight budget, and a funder or board asking for results.

This guide translates MSc-level project management theory — drawn from bodies of knowledge such as PMI’s PMBOK® Guide (PMI, 2021) and the APM Body of Knowledge (APM, 2019) — into short, practical, Irish-contextualised sections you can act on this week.

Who it is for

Built for two audiences

  • Small and micro businesses in Ireland running client projects, product launches, or internal change with limited resources.
  • Charities and community organisations delivering funded programmes, often with volunteer boards, restricted grants, and Charities Regulator obligations.

If you are managing a project without a project management qualification — this guide is for you.

Foundations of Project Management

What project management actually is

A project is a temporary undertaking with a defined start, end and outcome — as distinct from the ongoing, repeatable work of “operations” (PMI, 2021). Project management is the disciplined application of knowledge, skills, tools and techniques to meet a project’s objectives within agreed scope, time, cost and quality constraints (APM, 2019). For a small business that means: the new website, the funding-application-driven programme, the office move. For a charity it means: the six-month pilot service, the capital build, the new CRM roll-out.

The major frameworks, in plain English

PMBOK® (PMI, 2021) is a knowledge-area framework: it organises project work into domains such as scope, schedule, cost, quality, risk and stakeholders, and is flexible enough to sit under almost any delivery style.

PRINCE2 (Axelos, 2017) is a structured, stage-gated method built around a business case that must justify the project at every stage — useful where a board or funder needs formal sign-off points.

Agile (Beck et al., 2001) favours short, iterative cycles, early delivery of working outputs, and continuous feedback — well suited to projects where requirements will evolve, such as a new digital service.

Lean (Womack and Jones, 1996) focuses on eliminating waste and maximising value to the end user or beneficiary — a natural fit for resource-constrained charities.

None of these is “correct”. Most small organisations end up hybrid: a PRINCE2-style business case and stage gates for governance, delivered through short Agile-style sprints day to day.

The Irish context

Irish small businesses can draw on structured supports rarely covered in generic PM textbooks: Local Enterprise Offices (LEOs) and Enterprise Ireland both fund and mentor project-based growth activity, and increasingly require a project plan and milestones as part of any grant application (Enterprise Ireland, 2023). Irish registered charities operate under the Charities Act 2009 and are supervised by the Charities Regulator, which expects funded projects to be governed against the Charities Governance Code (Charities Regulator, 2018). Charities also commonly report to funders such as Pobal, the HSE, or philanthropic trusts, each with its own project reporting cycle — meaning Irish project managers typically manage to more than one accountability structure at once.

How teams actually form

Bruce Tuckman’s model describes teams moving through Forming, Storming, Norming and Performing, with a later-added Adjourning stage as the project closes (Tuckman, 1965; Tuckman and Jensen, 1977). Small Irish teams rarely move through these neatly — a volunteer board might sit at Storming for months, or a seasonal staff turnover can reset a team back to Forming mid-project. Naming the stage your team is in is often more useful than trying to rush past it.

Knowing who does what: Belbin

Meredith Belbin’s research identified nine team roles across three clusters — action-oriented (Shaper, Implementer, Completer-Finisher), people-oriented (Coordinator, Teamworker, Resource Investigator) and thought-oriented (Plant, Monitor Evaluator, Specialist) (Belbin, 2010). In a five-person charity team, most people will carry two or three roles at once. The value isn’t the label — it’s spotting the gaps, most often the Completer-Finisher role in small organisations that move fast and rarely close things out properly.

Leading a small, often part-time team

Transformational leadership motivates through shared vision and individual development (Bass, 1985); servant leadership prioritises removing obstacles for the team over directing it (Greenleaf, 1977); adaptive leadership treats leadership as a practice of mobilising people through difficult, non-technical challenges (Heifetz, Grashow and Linsky, 2009). Irish charities, where a manager often leads paid staff, volunteers and a board simultaneously, tend to need all three at different moments — vision-setting with the board, servant leadership with stretched staff, and adaptive leadership when a funding cut forces a hard reprioritisation.

Managing conflict without letting it fester

Thomas and Kilmann’s (1974) conflict-mode model sets out five responses — competing, collaborating, compromising, avoiding and accommodating — none inherently right, but each suited to different situations. In small teams, avoiding is the most common default and the most damaging over time: unresolved conflict between two long-standing volunteers, for instance, can outlast several project managers. A short, structured check-in cadence surfaces friction before it hardens.

Identifying risk before it identifies you

Risk is “an uncertain event or condition that, if it occurs, has a positive or negative effect on project objectives” (PMI, 2021). Identification techniques range from structured brainstorming and checklists to SWOT analysis and expert interviews (Hillson, 2009). For small organisations, the most reliable technique is simply scheduling a recurring 20-minute risk conversation — most risk goes unmanaged not because it’s unknown, but because no one owns the time to discuss it.

Analysing and prioritising

Once identified, risks are typically scored on likelihood and impact, often visualised on a probability–impact matrix (APM, 2019). This need not be sophisticated: a simple 1–5 scale on each axis, multiplied to give a priority score, is enough to focus a small team’s limited time on the handful of risks that matter most.

Responding: the four classic strategies

PMI (2021) sets out four negative-risk responses — avoid (eliminate the threat), mitigate (reduce likelihood or impact), transfer (insurance, contracts, sub-contracting) and accept (proceed and monitor, appropriate for low-priority risks). Each response should have a named owner — an unowned risk is, in practice, an unmanaged one.

Risk that is distinctly Irish, and distinctly small-organisation

Small businesses face concentration risk (a small client base), currency and Brexit-related supply risk, and cash-flow risk tied to slow payment cycles. Charities face funding-cycle risk (multi-year programmes funded year-to-year), volunteer dependency risk, and compliance risk under the Charities Governance Code (Charities Regulator, 2018) and GDPR as enforced by the Irish Data Protection Commission. Both sectors should treat key-person dependency — the risk of losing one critical staff member or volunteer — as a standing agenda item.

Ethics, Sustainability & Governance

The PMI Code of Ethics, the UN SDGs, and Irish charity and company governance requirements.

A professional code, not just good intentions

The PMI Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct commits project professionals to four values: responsibility, respect, fairness and honesty (PMI, 2021). These aren’t abstractions — in practice they mean things like disclosing a conflict of interest to a board before it affects a procurement decision, or being honest with a funder about a project falling behind schedule rather than quietly hoping to catch up.

Sustainability as a design constraint, not an add-on

The UN Sustainable Development Goals (United Nations, 2015) increasingly shape both business and charity funding criteria in Ireland, with Enterprise Ireland and many philanthropic funders now asking applicants to identify which SDGs a project advances. Treating sustainability — environmental, social and financial — as a design constraint from the outset, rather than a report written after the fact, tends to produce both better outcomes and stronger funding applications.

Governance: who is actually accountable

Governance is the system by which an organisation is directed and controlled (Cadbury, 1992). For Irish charities, this means compliance with the Charities Governance Code (Charities Regulator, 2018), which sets minimum standards across leading the organisation, exercising control, working effectively as a board, and behaving with integrity. For companies, the Companies Act 2014 sets director duties that apply directly to how projects are approved and funded. A project sponsor should always be able to name, in one sentence, who has authority to stop the project.

Strategy, Planning & Implementation

RegionIreland

LevelFoundation
Est. time7 min read
FormatText / audio / video

Starting from strategy, not from tasks

Every project should trace back to a strategic objective — Kaplan and Norton’s (1996) work on strategic alignment argues that initiatives disconnected from strategy consume resource without building organisational value. In practice: before opening a project plan, write one sentence linking the project to your organisation’s strategic plan or, for a charity, its stated charitable objects.

Breaking work down: the WBS

A Work Breakdown Structure (WBS) decomposes a project into progressively smaller, manageable components (PMI, 2021). The discipline is useful even at small scale: a WBS forces you to name every deliverable before estimating time or cost, catching missing work early rather than mid-project.

Scheduling and accountability: Gantt and RACI

Henry Gantt’s chart (Gantt, 1910; popularised through the 20th century) remains the clearest way to visualise sequence, duration and overlap of tasks. A RACI matrix (Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed) then assigns clarity to who does what — particularly valuable where a project spans staff, volunteers and a board, and ambiguity about accountability is a common cause of delay.

From plan to reality: implementation

Kotter’s (1996) eight-step model for leading change — from creating urgency through to anchoring new approaches in organisational culture — remains one of the most cited implementation frameworks, and applies as much to a ten-person charity adopting new case-management software as it does to a large corporate transformation. The common implementation failure in small organisations isn’t poor planning; it’s under-resourcing the transition itself — training, communication, and the temporary dip in productivity change always causes.

Why stakeholders decide whether a project succeeds

R. Edward Freeman’s (1984) stakeholder theory defines a stakeholder as any group or individual who can affect, or is affected by, an organisation’s objectives. Project success is rarely purely technical — a project delivered on time and on budget can still fail if the people it depends on, or affects, were never properly engaged (Bourne, 2009).

Mapping stakeholders: power and interest

The classic power–interest grid (Mendelow, 1981) plots stakeholders by how much influence they hold and how much interest they have in the project, guiding four broad engagement strategies: manage closely, keep satisfied, keep informed, and monitor. For Irish charities, a funder typically sits in “manage closely” even when day-to-day interest is low, purely because of the power they hold over continuation funding.

Building a communication plan that people actually read

A communication plan should specify, for each key stakeholder group, the information they need, the channel, the frequency, and who is responsible for sending it (PMI, 2021). Over-communication to low-interest stakeholders is as damaging as under-communication to high-power ones — both waste attention that could go where it matters.

Irish stakeholder groups worth naming explicitly

Irish charity projects typically juggle: the board of trustees (governance and legal accountability), funders (financial control and reporting requirements), volunteers (delivery capacity, but with limited formal obligation to the organisation), service users or beneficiaries, and increasingly the Charities Regulator as a compliance stakeholder. Small businesses should equally name clients, suppliers, and — where relevant — Revenue and the Companies Registration Office as compliance stakeholders whose expectations shape the project.

Methodologies

Waterfall: sequential and predictable

The waterfall approach — plan fully, then execute in sequential phases — traces to Royce’s (1970) description of sequential software development. It suits projects with fixed, well-understood requirements: a capital build, a compliance-driven system roll-out, or any project where a funder requires a fixed scope and budget agreed up front.

Agile: Scrum and Kanban

Scrum (Schwaber and Sutherland, 2020) organises work into fixed-length sprints with defined roles (Product Owner, Scrum Master, Development Team) and ceremonies (planning, daily stand-up, review, retrospective). Kanban (Anderson, 2010) instead visualises flow on a board of columns — typically To Do, In Progress, Done — and limits how much work is in progress at once, without fixed sprint lengths. Kanban tends to suit small, resource-constrained teams better than Scrum, since it requires less ceremony and role overhead.

Hybrid: the honest default for most small organisations

In practice, most Irish SMEs and charities run a hybrid model: a waterfall-style business case and milestone structure to satisfy a funder or board, with Agile or Kanban-style delivery underneath to manage the actual day-to-day work (Conforto et al., 2014). There is no methodological purity prize for choosing one framework exclusively — the right test is whether your chosen approach matches your funder’s reporting needs and your team’s working reality.

AI in Project Management

Where AI genuinely saves a small team time, and how to use it responsibly.

RegionIreland
TopicAI in Project Management

Where AI genuinely helps a small team

Recent research on AI in project management points to three consistently useful applications: automated scheduling and resource levelling, predictive risk flagging from historical project data, and administrative automation — meeting summarisation, status-report drafting, and data entry (PMI, 2023; Auth, Jokinen and Pasian, 2023). For a two-person project office, automating admin work often returns more time than any other single change.

Low-cost tools accessible to Irish SMEs and charities

Many everyday tools now embed AI project features at low or no additional cost: AI-assisted scheduling within tools such as Asana, Trello and Monday.com; meeting-transcription and summary tools; and general-purpose assistants such as Claude or ChatGPT for drafting funding reports, risk registers, or communication plans from a set of bullet points. The barrier for most small Irish organisations is not cost but time to learn the tool — budgeting a short onboarding session pays back quickly.

Using AI ethically and responsibly

The PMI Code of Ethics’ principle of honesty extends directly to AI use: outputs should be checked, not assumed accurate, and their use disclosed where they materially shape a funding report or decision (PMI, 2021). Under GDPR, Irish charities and businesses must take particular care not to input personal or sensitive data — service-user details, health information, staff records — into public AI tools without a clear lawful basis and appropriate data processing terms (Data Protection Commission, 2023). Treat AI as a fast first draft, always reviewed by a human before it reaches a funder, client or board.
