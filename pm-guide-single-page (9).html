Purpose of this guide

Why this exists

Most project management advice is written for large corporates with dedicated PMOs, enterprise software and full time project managers. Irish small businesses and charities rarely have any of that, instead they have a manager wearing five hats, a handful of staff or volunteers, a tight budget, and a funder or board asking for results.

This guide translates MSc level project management theory, drawn from bodies of knowledge such as PMI's PMBOK® Guide (PMI, 2021) and the APM Body of Knowledge (APM, 2019), into short, practical, Irish contextualised sections you can act on this week. Each section also names the real academic critique of its own tools, not just the theory itself.

Who it is for

Built for two audiences

  • Small and micro businesses in Ireland running client projects, product launches, or internal change with limited resources.
  • Charities and community organisations delivering funded programmes, often with volunteer boards, restricted grants, and Charities Regulator obligations.

If you are managing a project without a project management qualification, this guide is for you.

Foundations of Project Management

What project management actually is

A project is a temporary undertaking with a defined start, end and outcome, distinct from the ongoing, repeatable work of operations (PMI, 2021). Project management is the disciplined application of knowledge, skills, tools and techniques to meet a project's objectives within agreed scope, time, cost and quality constraints (APM, 2019).

Most treatments of this stop there, but a genuinely rigorous starting point needs to place the project inside the wider structures it sits within. PMI (2021) and Turner (2014) distinguish three levels: a project (a single temporary undertaking), a programme (a group of related projects managed together for benefits not available from managing them individually), and a portfolio (an organisation's entire collection of projects and programmes, prioritised against strategy). A charity's digital transformation is rarely one project, it is a programme of several: a new website, a CRM migration, staff training.

Underpinning all three levels is the classic triple constraint, scope, time and cost, later extended to quality, risk and benefits (PMI, 2021), the central tension every framework below exists to manage. Fix two of these, and the third will always move.

A brief history, why these frameworks exist at all

Project management as a formal discipline emerged from specific pressures, not in the abstract. Gantt's scheduling charts (1910) and the US Navy's development of PERT in the 1950s grew out of large defence and engineering programmes needing to coordinate thousands of interdependent tasks. PMI was founded in 1969 to professionalise the discipline, PRINCE2 emerged from UK government IT projects in 1989, built for environments demanding formal accountability. The Agile Manifesto (Beck et al., 2001) was a direct reaction against those heavyweight, plan driven methods. Lean (Womack and Jones, 1996) came out of Toyota's manufacturing system, focused on eliminating waste.

The point worth making explicitly: every framework was built to solve a specific problem in a specific context, and none of those contexts was a five person Irish charity.

The frameworks, and a genuine critique of each

PMBOK® (PMI, 2021) organises project work into knowledge areas, scope, schedule, cost, quality, risk, stakeholders, flexible enough to sit under almost any delivery style, but offering no methodology of its own.

PRINCE2 (Axelos, 2017) is stage gated and business case driven, built for environments where a board or funder needs formal sign off at every stage.

Agile (Beck et al., 2001) favours short iterative cycles and continuous stakeholder feedback, well suited to evolving requirements, but it assumes a level of stakeholder availability that a stretched five person team, or a board that meets quarterly, often cannot provide.

Lean (Womack and Jones, 1996) focuses on eliminating waste and maximising value to the end user, a natural fit for resource constrained charities, though it offers few concrete delivery mechanics on its own.

Here is the critical layer that moves this beyond textbook description. A body of critical project management scholarship, notably the UK's EPSRC funded Rethinking Project Management research network, has argued that mainstream bodies of knowledge like PMBOK and PRINCE2 are built on assumptions of rational, controllable, well resourced environments that rarely match how projects actually unfold in practice (Cicmil, Williams, Thomas and Hodgson, 2006; Winter, Smith, Morris and Cicmil, 2006). Cicmil et al. (2006) argue these frameworks are essentially positivist, they treat projects as controllable and decoupled from their messy organisational context, when the lived reality of project work is far more improvised and constrained by the resources actually available.

This lands with particular force for Irish SMEs and charities. A rigidly applied PRINCE2 process, with its formal stage boundaries and documentation overhead, assumes a level of dedicated project management resource a two person office simply does not have. This is the actual argument for the hybrid approach recommended throughout this guide, not hybrid because it is easier, but hybrid because the evidence on framework context fit demands it.

Why this matters, the cost of getting it wrong

PMI's 2023 Pulse of the Profession survey, a global survey of thousands of project professionals, found organisations waste an average of 5.2% of every euro invested in projects due to poor project performance (PMI, 2023). At Irish enterprise scale, that is a meaningful, recoverable loss for organisations that can least afford it.

CSO data show that of enterprises born in Ireland in 2018, only 47.6% were still active five years later in 2023 (CSO, 2024). SMEs make up 99.8% of all enterprises in Ireland and 67.9% of all persons employed (CSO, 2023), meaning the health of Irish SMEs is, in large part, the health of the Irish economy. Poorly managed projects are a genuine contributor to that failure rate.

The project lifecycle

Regardless of framework, almost every project moves through five broad phases (PMI, 2021): initiation, planning, execution, monitoring and control, and closure. This lifecycle is the connective tissue running through every later section of this guide, Risk and Stakeholders sit mostly in planning and monitoring, Strategy and Implementation span initiation through closure.

Is this actually a project? A quick decision check

Before applying any of the above, it is worth confirming you are looking at a project at all.

  • Does it have a defined end date, or is it ongoing indefinitely? Ongoing means operations, not a project.
  • Will it produce a specific, identifiable outcome?
  • Is it distinct enough from business as usual to warrant its own plan and budget line?

If you answered yes to all three, you have a project, and everything in this guide applies.

The Irish context, in more depth

Enterprise Ireland's Competitive Start Fund and Local Enterprise Office grants each require a structured project plan with milestones as part of the application. Funders increasingly expect the vocabulary of project management, scope, milestones, risk, even from very small applicants. On the charity side, the Charities Regulator's Governance Code (2018) expects funded activity to be governed with clear oversight, a project charter and named accountable owner is close to a de facto requirement for compliant delivery.

Ireland also has real professional infrastructure behind this. The PMI Ireland Chapter, with over 1,400 members, runs regular events and training across the country, and APM's Chartered Project Professional standard is gaining traction here as a marker of senior competency.

How teams actually form

Bruce Tuckman's model describes teams moving through Forming, Storming, Norming and Performing, with a later added Adjourning stage as the project closes (Tuckman, 1965; Tuckman and Jensen, 1977).

It is worth being honest about what this model actually is. Tuckman's original 1965 paper was a review of existing group dynamics literature, not a new empirical study of real working teams. That does not make it useless, decades of practitioner experience back up the general pattern. But the stages should be treated as a useful description, not a proven law every team will follow in order.

Small Irish teams rarely move through these neatly. A volunteer board might sit at Storming for months, or seasonal staff turnover can reset a team back to Forming mid project. Naming the stage your team is in is often more useful than trying to rush past it.

Why teams get stuck at Storming, psychological safety

A concept largely missing from most Tuckman based advice explains why so many teams get stuck rather than moving through Storming, psychological safety.

Psychological safety is defined by Amy Edmondson as a shared belief among team members that the team is safe for interpersonal risk taking (Edmondson, 1999). Edmondson's original study of 51 work teams found psychological safety was the strongest predictor of team learning behaviour, more than team confidence or efficacy.

This finding was later echoed at scale by Google's own internal research, known as Project Aristotle, which studied 180 teams and found psychological safety was the single strongest predictor of team effectiveness, ahead of who was actually on the team.

For a small charity board or a mixed staff and volunteer team, this has a direct practical implication. Conflict does not go away because nobody raises it, it goes underground. A team that never appears to reach Storming has often simply made it unsafe to disagree.

Knowing who does what, Belbin, and a fair critique of it

Meredith Belbin's research identified nine team roles across three clusters: action oriented (Shaper, Implementer, Completer Finisher), people oriented (Coordinator, Teamworker, Resource Investigator) and thought oriented (Plant, Monitor Evaluator, Specialist) (Belbin, 2010). It remains one of the most widely used team tools in practice, including across FTSE 100 companies (Aritzeta, Swailes and Senior, 2007).

It is also worth knowing that Belbin's psychometric tool has faced real academic criticism. Furnham, Steele and Pendleton (1993) tested the Belbin Self Perception Inventory directly and found weak internal consistency and no clear evidence for the proposed nine factor structure. A later, more balanced review by Aritzeta, Swailes and Senior (2007), covering 43 empirical studies, concluded the evidence is mixed, the model has adequate convergent validity overall, but weak discriminant validity between some of the roles.

The honest takeaway for a small organisation, treat Belbin as a useful shared vocabulary for spotting gaps in a team, not as a scientifically validated instrument for hiring or formal assessment decisions.

Leading a small, often part time team

Transformational leadership motivates through shared vision and individual development (Bass, 1985). Servant leadership prioritises removing obstacles for the team over directing it (Greenleaf, 1977). Adaptive leadership treats leadership as a practice of mobilising people through difficult, non technical challenges (Heifetz, Grashow and Linsky, 2009).

Irish charities, where a manager often leads paid staff, volunteers and a board simultaneously, tend to need all three at different moments. Vision setting with the board. Servant leadership with a stretched team. Adaptive leadership when a funding cut forces a hard reprioritisation.

Leading employees and volunteers are not the same job

This is a distinction most generic project management guides skip entirely, and it matters enormously in an Irish context.

Volunteers are not covered by the same employment protections as paid staff under Irish employment law. This means a manager cannot performance manage a volunteer the way they would an employee, there is no disciplinary process, no contractual obligation to turn up, and no pay to withhold.

Leadership of a volunteer has to work almost entirely through the mechanisms above, shared vision, psychological safety, removing obstacles, because the usual levers of employment are simply not available. Given how central volunteers are to the Irish community and voluntary sector, this is not a minor caveat, it is often the central leadership challenge.

Managing conflict without letting it fester

Thomas and Kilmann's (1974) conflict mode model sets out five responses, competing, collaborating, compromising, avoiding and accommodating, none inherently right, but each suited to different situations.

In small teams, avoiding is the most common default, and the most damaging over time. Unresolved conflict between two long standing volunteers can outlast several project managers. A short, structured check in cadence, paired with the psychological safety practices above, surfaces friction before it hardens.

The Irish context, volunteering by the numbers

Volunteering is not a marginal activity in Ireland, it is a structural part of how projects and services actually get delivered. CSO Census 2022 data found 711,379 people, 14% of the population, regularly engaged in voluntary work, with the average volunteer aged 47, around eight years older than the general population (CSO, 2024).

More recent CSO Labour Force Survey data from 2025 puts the figure even higher, at 16.1% of people aged 15 to 74, contributing over 7.5 million volunteer hours a month nationally (CSO, 2026). For any Irish charity, this means the pool of available volunteers skews older, and any team plan built only around younger, digitally fluent volunteers is planning against the actual demographic reality.

Identifying risk before it identifies you

Risk is an uncertain event or condition that, if it occurs, has a positive or negative effect on project objectives (PMI, 2021). The international standard for this, ISO 31000, frames risk management as a continuous cycle, identify, analyse, evaluate, treat, then monitor and review (ISO, 2018).

For small organisations, the technique matters less than the habit. Most risk goes unmanaged not because it is unknown, but because no one owns the time to talk about it. A recurring 20 minute risk conversation catches more than any elaborate framework.

The 5x5 matrix, and why you should use it with your eyes open

Once identified, risks are typically scored on likelihood and impact, usually on a grid multiplied to give a priority score (APM, 2019). This is the most common risk tool in the world, and it is worth knowing it has a real, well documented flaw.

Cox (2008), writing in the academic journal Risk Analysis, showed mathematically that these grids can misrank risks, two very different risks can land in the same box, and in some cases a matrix will rate a smaller risk as more urgent than a genuinely bigger one. His conclusion was not to abandon them, it was to use them with caution, and be honest that the scores are a structured discussion, not a precise measurement.

Risk appetite and risk tolerance are not the same thing

Two terms worth having a real conversation with your board about, because they get used interchangeably and should not be.

Risk appetite is how much risk your organisation is willing to take on in pursuit of its goals. Risk tolerance is how much risk you can actually absorb before it threatens survival (ISO, 2018). A charity might have a high appetite for trying a bold new programme, but very low tolerance for financial risk if it only has three months of reserves. Naming both explicitly, ideally once a year with the board, makes every later risk decision faster.

Responding, the four classic strategies

PMI (2021) sets out four responses to a negative risk: avoid it entirely, mitigate it by reducing likelihood or impact, transfer it through insurance or a contract, or accept it and monitor. Every response needs a named owner, a risk with no owner is, in practice, unmanaged.

What structured risk registers are blind to

One honest limitation worth naming: a risk register is only as good as the risks someone thought to write down. Writer and former options trader Nassim Nicholas Taleb popularised the term black swan for exactly this problem, rare, high impact events that fall outside what anyone expected to plan for (Taleb, 2007). The practical response is not to abandon risk registers, it is to pair them with financial resilience, reserves and diversified funding, that can absorb a shock nobody saw coming.

Why this matters, with real numbers behind it

PMI's 2023 global Pulse of the Profession survey found organisations waste an average of 5.2% of every euro invested in projects due to poor performance, a figure that includes poor risk management as a major driver (PMI, 2023).

Ireland has its own sobering case study. The 2016 Console scandal, where a suicide support charity's founder had quietly diverted close to 500,000 euro in charity funds for personal spending, and the 2013 to 2014 Central Remedial Clinic pay controversy, badly damaged public trust in the sector, a survey found over 97% of Irish charities believed the CRC scandal alone had hurt confidence, and over half said it directly hit their own fundraising (RTE, 2024; Catholicireland.net, 2014). Both scandals directly triggered the activation of the Charities Regulator's full investigative powers under the Charities Act 2009 (Charities Regulator, 2018). Unmanaged reputational and governance risk in a couple of organisations reshaped regulation for the entire sector.

Risk that is distinctly small organisation, and distinctly Irish

Small businesses face concentration risk from a narrow client base, and cash flow risk tied to slow payment cycles. Charities face funding cycle risk, since many programmes are funded year to year rather than long term, and volunteer dependency risk.

One practical, commonly overlooked risk, public liability insurance for events. It is not a legal requirement in Ireland, but most venues will ask to see proof of it before allowing a charity or community group to book a space, and it typically does not automatically cover volunteers unless the policy specifically extends to them, that needs to be checked, not assumed.

Both sectors should also treat key person dependency, the risk of losing one critical staff member or volunteer, as a standing item on every risk review, not a one off entry.

LevelFoundation

What ethics actually means here

Ethics is a set of moral principles, or the principles of conduct governing an individual or group (Merriam-Webster, cited in Houghton, 2013). For project managers this is not abstract philosophy. It shows up in specific moments, what you disclose to a board, how you treat a supplier, whether you flag a problem before it becomes a crisis.

Project management often sits in genuine grey areas. As practitioner Kay Wais has pointed out, one of PMBOK's own recommended risk responses is called Exploit, ensuring a positive opportunity actually happens. Taught badly, that can sound like licence to exploit people rather than situations. Whether it is actually carried out ethically depends entirely on the personal integrity of the practitioner (Wais, cited in Houghton, 2013).

The PMI Code of Ethics, properly explained

PMI's Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct rests on four values, decided by surveying the global project management community: responsibility, respect, fairness and honesty (PMI, 2021).

Each value has two levels. Aspirational standards describe the conduct practitioners strive for, not easily measured, but still an expectation, not optional. Mandatory standards are firm requirements, breach them and you are subject to disciplinary procedures, for PMI members that means the Ethics Review Committee (PMI, 2021).

A few mandatory standards worth knowing by heart: disclose any real or potential conflict of interest, and step back from decisions until it is resolved. Never make hiring, firing, or contract decisions based on favouritism or bribery. Never mislead through false statements, half truths, or withholding context. Report unethical or illegal conduct to appropriate management.

Ethics is not just about individuals, it is about the system around them

A common assumption is that ethics is primarily a leadership responsibility, tone at the top. Hess and Broughton (2014), writing in Business Horizons, challenge this directly. They argue organisations need an ethical infrastructure, both formal systems, codes, training, whistleblower channels, and informal ones, open conversation, everyday norms, because informal culture often does more to prevent misconduct than policy alone.

Two findings are genuinely useful for a small Irish organisation. First, employee tips are by far the most effective way frauds get uncovered, detecting 43.3% of cases compared to 14.6% for management review, far ahead of audits (Association of Certified Fraud Examiners, 2012, cited in Hess and Broughton, 2014). Second, nearly half of all fraud tips come from outside the organisation entirely, customers, suppliers, service users, not staff. For a charity, that means your funders and service users are a genuine early warning system, if there is an accessible way for them to speak up.

Even the language you use shapes ethical behaviour

In a controlled study, participants playing an identical game behaved far more cooperatively when it was called The Community Game than when the exact same rules were called The Wall Street Game (Liberman, Samuels and Ross, 2004, cited in Hess and Broughton, 2014). Nothing about the task changed, only the framing. The words an organisation uses about its own work, competitive versus collaborative, transactional versus mission driven, measurably shape how people behave inside it.

What sustainability actually means, and why the definition is contested

The most widely used definition comes from the 1987 Brundtland Report, development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs. Elliott (2012), in her widely used academic text, is upfront that sustainable development is a genuinely contested, frequently over-used term, with no single blueprint. What it means is specific to a particular organisation, place, and moment in time.

That is useful news for a small Irish organisation. You do not need a corporate scale sustainability strategy. You need an honest, specific answer to what sustainability means for your project, stated in the brief from day one rather than added afterwards as a report.

Adopted by all 193 UN member states in 2015, the SDGs are a shared, interconnected set of global targets for 2030 (United Nations, 2015).

What governance actually means

Project governance is the framework of rules, roles, relationships and processes that ensure projects are delivered effectively, transparently, and in line with organisational goals (Mallin, 2010). Day to day project management is about how work gets done, deadlines, tasks, who is assigned what. Governance is about oversight and accountability, who has the authority to decide, how budgets and risks are actually monitored, and whether the project stays within legal, ethical and organisational bounds.

Turning the Charities Governance Code into a working dashboard

Every registered Irish charity is expected to meet the Charities Governance Code (Charities Regulator, 2018), six principles, 32 core standards, and 17 additional standards for larger organisations. Most small organisations treat this as a document to file away. It works far better as something your actual project board reflects.

A few principles translate directly into a Trello, Asana or Notion setup. Principle 1, Advancing Charitable Purpose, link every project template directly to your charity's core mission milestones. Principle 4, Exercising Control, build a required approval step into every board for budget sign off. Principle 6, Being Accountable and Transparent, keep a single, central, dated document hub for meeting minutes and trustee decisions.

Role clarity with RACI

Small organisations often fail at governance not through bad intentions but because roles are fuzzy, nobody is sure who actually has to sign off on something. Building a simple RACI matrix directly into your project tool fixes this, Responsible is the person doing the work, Accountable is the one person who signs off the final deliverable, Consulted are subject matter experts asked before a decision, Informed are those who need to know progress but do not need to be asked.

The critical governance point, only one person should ever be Accountable for a given decision. The moment two people are both marked Accountable, nobody actually is.

Decision gateways, stopping scope creep before it starts

Visual approval stages, built directly into a Kanban style board in Trello, ClickUp or Monday.com, prevent unauthorised spending and scope creep by making every project pass through the same checkpoints: Idea or Proposal, Board or Manager Approval, Fund Allocation, Execution. A card cannot move from one column to the next until whoever holds that stage's authority physically drags it there.

A lightweight risk and compliance register

Beyond the general risk register covered earlier in this guide, a small compliance specific board is worth keeping separate. Data protection, log what personal data each project touches and your file retention period, directly on the relevant task card. Funding compliance, tag expenditure against the specific grant it belongs to, so an audit ready breakdown can be produced instantly.

Worth being precise about which funding streams apply where. The LEO Grow Digital Voucher, up to 5,000 euro, 50% funded, requiring a prior Digital for Business assessment, is a small business scheme and explicitly excludes charities and non-profits (Local Enterprise Office, 2026). For the community and voluntary sector, the equivalent routes are Pobal administered programmes and LEADER, the EU funded rural development programme delivered through Local Action Groups.

Companies Act 2014, the eight statutory director duties

For businesses, and for charities structured as companies limited by guarantee, Section 228 of the Companies Act 2014 sets out eight core statutory duties owed by every director (Government of Ireland, 2014): act in good faith, act honestly and responsibly, act within the company's constitution, not misuse company property or information, not restrict independent judgement without authorisation, avoid conflicts of interest, exercise reasonable care and skill, and have regard to employees' interests. A 2022 amendment added a further duty regarding creditors' interests once a company is insolvent or approaching it.

What actually distinguishes a working board from a rubber stamp

Kirwan, McBride and O'Riordan (2018), in Cases in Corporate Governance and Business Ethics, work through real Irish governance failures. The pattern that recurs, a board that trusted rather than verified, with nobody structurally independent enough to ask a hard question in time.

In one real case, the Charities Regulator found that the CEO of the charity Ataxia Ireland had paid 84,000 euro to her own parents, who were also the charity's trustees, contrary to Revenue's position that trustees should be unpaid (PressReader, 2018). The failure was not one bad actor, it was a board with no independent oversight to catch it. That is exactly why single owner accountability, visible approval gates, and a documented decisions log exist, not as bureaucracy, but as the structural independence a rubber stamp board is missing.

Starting from strategy, not tasks

Every project should trace back to a strategic objective. Kaplan and Norton (1996) argue that initiatives disconnected from strategy consume resources without building real value. Before opening a project plan, write one sentence linking the project to your strategic plan, or for a charity, its charitable objects.

Breaking work down, the WBS

A Work Breakdown Structure decomposes a project into smaller, manageable pieces (PMI, 2021). It is useful even at small scale. It forces you to name every deliverable before estimating time or cost, catching missing work early rather than mid project.

Scheduling and accountability, Gantt and RACI

A Gantt chart visualises sequence, duration and overlap of tasks. A RACI matrix then makes clear who does what, especially valuable when a project spans staff, volunteers and a board.

Why projects run over time and budget, and it is not bad luck

Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky, whose work later won Kahneman the Nobel Prize, identified the planning fallacy, people underestimate time, cost and risk while overestimating benefits, even with direct experience of past overruns (Kahneman and Tversky, 1979).

The cause is what they call the inside view. When planning, people focus on their own project's best case path, not on how similar projects actually turned out. Bent Flyvbjerg's research on large infrastructure projects, spanning 70 years, found average cost overruns of 45% on rail projects, and up to 238% in some categories. The pattern has barely changed across seven decades (Flyvbjerg, Holm and Buhl, 2002).

The fix is called the outside view, or reference class forecasting, find a few genuinely comparable past projects, and use their actual outcomes as your starting estimate. For a small organisation, this does not need to be sophisticated. Before finalising a budget, ask, how long did our last comparable project actually take, not how long we planned for it to take.

What Irish funders actually want to see, the logic model

Corporate tools like the Balanced Scorecard are built for large organisations. Most Irish funders, including Pobal and the HSE, expect something simpler, the logic model. It is a straight chain, inputs, activities, outputs, outcomes. It forces you to define success as a real change for people, not just a completed activity.

From plan to reality

A plan is not the same as change actually happening. Most implementation failure in small organisations is not caused by poor planning, it is caused by under resourcing the transition itself, training, communication, and the temporary dip in productivity that any real change causes.

Kotter's eight steps

John Kotter's (1996) model remains one of the most cited implementation frameworks: create urgency, build a coalition, form a vision, communicate it, remove obstacles, generate short term wins, build on momentum, anchor the change in culture.

Worth knowing, Kotter later revised his own model, moving from a strict sequence to eight accelerators that can run at once (Kotter, 2014). Real change is messier than a neat staircase, better to expect that from the start.

The half of implementation Kotter does not cover, the individual

Kotter answers what must the organisation do. It does not answer what does each person actually need. That is the gap ADKAR fills, developed by Prosci founder Jeff Hiatt after studying over 700 organisations (Hiatt, 2006). Five building blocks, in sequence: Awareness of why the change is needed, Desire to participate, Knowledge of how to change, Ability to actually do it, Reinforcement to make it stick.

If a change stalls, ADKAR gives a real diagnostic, which of these five is actually missing. Often it is not Knowledge, people know what to do, it is Desire, nobody explained what is in it for them.

Why this matters more for volunteer led organisations

Kotter and ADKAR were both built with paid staff in mind, people who can ultimately be required to change. A volunteer can simply stop turning up. That makes Awareness and Desire disproportionately important for Irish charities, you cannot mandate adoption of a new system, you have to earn it.

Why stakeholders decide whether a project succeeds

R. Edward Freeman's (1984) stakeholder theory defines a stakeholder as any group or individual who can affect, or is affected by, an organisation's objectives. Project success is rarely purely technical, a project delivered on time and on budget can still fail if the people it depends on, or affects, were never properly engaged.

Mapping stakeholders, power and interest, but treat it as a snapshot

The classic power interest grid plots stakeholders by how much influence they hold and how much interest they have, guiding four engagement strategies: manage closely, keep satisfied, keep informed, monitor.

Here is the honest limitation. This grid captures a single moment. Mitchell, Agle and Wood (1997), in one of the most cited papers in management theory, argue that a stakeholder's real priority depends on three shifting attributes, not two: power, legitimacy, and urgency. A stakeholder with low power and low interest today can suddenly demand real attention the moment something urgent happens to them, a service disruption, a funding announcement. Their model calls this stakeholder salience, and the key insight is that it moves.

The practical fix, keep the map alive, not filed away

Most stakeholder maps get built once, at kickoff, then never opened again. A shared document, kept live through the project rather than archived after the first workshop, does far more good than a beautifully designed one off diagram. Revisit it at every major milestone, and ask explicitly, has anyone's urgency changed.

Building a communication plan that people actually read

A communication plan should specify, for each key stakeholder group, the information they need, the channel, the frequency, and who is responsible for sending it (PMI, 2021). Over-communication to low interest stakeholders is as damaging as under communication to high power ones, both waste attention that could go where it matters.

A genuine shift in who counts as a stakeholder in Ireland

The HSE has moved decisively toward treating people with lived experience, service users, patients, carers, not as people to report to, but as active partners in designing services from the start (HSE, 2024). This is now formal HSE policy through its Patient and Public Partnership work, and it is spreading into how Pobal and other Irish funders assess project applications, co-design and consultation with the people a service actually affects is increasingly expected, not optional.

For a small Irish organisation, your service users belong on the stakeholder map not just as informed, at the bottom of the grid, but often as a stakeholder to consult and involve from the design stage, alongside your funders and board.

RegionIreland

Waterfall, sequential and predictable

Waterfall traces to Royce's (1970) description of sequential software development, plan fully, then execute in phases. It suits projects with fixed, well understood requirements, a capital build, a compliance driven system roll out, or any project where a funder needs scope and budget agreed upfront. If a funder needs a fixed budget approved before a euro is spent, Waterfall's rigidity is a feature, not a flaw.

Agile, Scrum and Kanban

Scrum organises work into fixed length sprints with defined roles and ceremonies. Kanban instead visualises flow on a board of columns, typically To Do, In Progress, Done, limiting how much work is in progress at once, without fixed sprint lengths. Kanban tends to suit small, resource constrained teams better than Scrum, since it needs far less ceremony and role overhead.

The evidence, and why it is not as clean as it sounds

Agile is often presented as simply the better methodology. The Standish Group's long running research does show Agile software projects succeeding roughly three times more often than Waterfall ones (Standish Group, 2020). That headline gets quoted constantly, and it is real. But two honest caveats matter.

First, even in Agile's best year, only around 40% of Agile projects actually succeed outright. Most are still challenged in some way. Second, and more useful, Standish's own research found the strongest predictors of success were not the methodology at all. They were a genuinely skilled team, an engaged sponsor, and the right culture (Standish Group, 2020). Methodology matters less than who is doing the work, and whether they are actually supported.

Why Agile assumes things most small Irish organisations do not have

Turk, France and Rumpe (2002), in a widely cited academic paper, identified what Agile methods actually depend on to work, a team that is co-located or readily available, dedicated time rather than work squeezed between other jobs, and experienced practitioners able to self-organise without heavy oversight.

Now picture a typical Irish charity project team, a coordinator working three days a week, two volunteers who show up when they can, a board member consulted by email once a month. None of Agile's founding assumptions hold. Adopting Scrum's full ceremony set onto a team like that often produces what practitioners call Agile theatre, all the meetings, none of the actual flexibility, because the conditions Agile needs were never there.

Kanban avoids most of this trap. No fixed meeting cadence, much lighter touch. That is a real, practical reason to default to it over Scrum in a small Irish organisation.

Lean, specifically for a charity, not a factory

Lean's origins are in Toyota's manufacturing system (Womack and Jones, 1996), but its actual insight travels well beyond factories, define what your beneficiary genuinely values, and cut whatever activity does not contribute to that. For an Irish charity, waste rarely looks like a factory floor. It looks like a grant report duplicated across three different formats for three different funders, a volunteer induction process that takes four sessions when it could take one, or a referral process where a service user is asked for the same information at every stage.

Where AI genuinely helps a small team

Automated scheduling and resource levelling, predictive risk flagging from historical data, and administrative automation, meeting summaries, status report drafting, data entry, are the three places AI consistently proves useful in project delivery (PMI, 2023).

For a two person project office, automating admin work usually returns more time than anything else. PMI's own 2025 research found only around 20% of project professionals report strong practical AI skills (PMI, 2025). Most people using these tools are still figuring it out.

A plain English guide to what is actually out there

Type of toolWhat it is genuinely good atWhere it can quietly go wrong
General AI assistants (chat based)Drafting reports from bullet points, summarising meeting notesCan state something false with total confidence
AI features in project toolsSuggesting task assignments, flagging overdue itemsOnly as good as the data already in the tool
Meeting transcription toolsTurning a recording into text and a summaryCan misattribute who said what
Spreadsheet AI add-onsSpotting patterns, drafting formulasCan invent a formula that looks right but is not

The risk this guide has to be honest about, confidently wrong answers

AI tools can produce a completely wrong answer, an invented statistic, a risk that never happened, a budget figure that is simply incorrect, and present it with exactly the same confident tone as a correct one. This is called hallucination, a known, structural feature of how these tools work, not a rare glitch.

A risk register with one invented risk sitting alongside real ones. A funder report with a figure nobody actually checked. A meeting summary that puts words in someone's mouth they never said. The rule has to be absolute, every AI generated figure, fact, or quote gets checked against the real source before it goes anywhere near a funder, client, or board.

Using AI ethically and responsibly

The PMI Code of Ethics' principle of honesty extends directly to AI use, outputs should be checked, not assumed accurate, and their use disclosed where they materially shape a funding report or decision (PMI, 2021).

Under GDPR, Irish charities and businesses must take particular care not to input personal or sensitive data, service user details, health information, staff records, into public AI tools without a clear lawful basis (Data Protection Commission, 2023).

The EU AI Act, what actually applies to a small Irish organisation

The EU AI Act classifies AI systems by risk level, unacceptable (banned outright), high risk (heavily regulated), limited risk (must disclose you are using AI), minimal risk (largely unregulated) (European Commission, 2024).

Everyday use of a general AI assistant to draft a report or summarise a meeting sits in minimal risk, no heavy compliance burden attached. The obligation worth knowing is AI literacy, in force since February 2025, which expects organisations using AI professionally to ensure staff have a basic understanding of what the tool does and its limits.

Where it gets serious, using AI to make automated decisions about people, screening job applicants, assessing eligibility for a service, anything touching biometric data. That lands in the high risk category with real obligations attached. Very few small organisations are doing this, but the line is worth knowing.

Why adoption speed matters here, and for whom

The average Irish volunteer is 47, older than the general population. AI adoption advice that assumes everyone finds new software intuitive is planning against the wrong demographic. Roll out any AI tool gradually, find someone patient enough to show a colleague or volunteer the same three steps twice, and treat digital comfort as something to build for, not assume.

The LEO Grow Digital Voucher can fund up to 5,000 euro, 50% of costs, toward either subscription, after a Digital for Business assessment.

Charity / community group

Start with Notion and Miro

Notion suits a charity board needing the Decisions Log and Governance Code tracking described in the Governance section, meeting minutes, trustee decisions and reports all in one locked, searchable place. Miro suits a mixed staff and volunteer team building things together from different locations, the Stakeholder Map, the power interest grid, the WBS tree, all genuinely better as a live shared board than a static document.

Both have strong free tiers, useful given the Grow Digital Voucher explicitly excludes charities, so these need to be funded from general operating budgets or Pobal grants instead.

If you are small enough that one person does everything, staff or volunteer, skip the board and workflow tools for now. Canva alone covers the most common need, funder reports and event graphics, and a single shared Notion page can hold your Risk Register and Decisions Log without needing a paid plan at all.
